Nobody Is Watching Your Stand-Up Clips. Here's Why.

You closed strong on Thursday. The room was full, the laughs were real, and you got the whole thing on video. You posted the best minute Friday morning. By Sunday it had 214 views, and you recognized most of the names in the likes: comics from the show, your roommate, a guy from your old job. Meanwhile some comic you've never heard of posted a clip shot in a worse room with a worse joke, and it has 900,000 views.

The tempting explanation is the algorithm. The algorithm hates stand-up, the algorithm buries small accounts, the algorithm only rewards crowd work and TikTok dances. Comforting, and wrong. The algorithm showed your clip to a few hundred people as a test. They swiped away in under two seconds, so it stopped showing it. The algorithm didn't reject your comedy. It never saw your comedy, and neither did they. They saw the first two seconds of your video, and the first two seconds of your video were you adjusting the mic stand.

A set is built for a room. A clip is built for a thumb.

Live stand-up earns attention once, at the top of the show, and then holds it. You can take your time. You can lay two minutes of pipe because the audience has paid, sat down, and agreed to wait for the payoff. That agreement is the whole architecture of a live set, and it does not exist on a phone. On a phone you are auditioning for a stranger's attention every single second, against every other video on earth, and the stranger owes you nothing.

Most comics film the set and post the set. That means the clip inherits the structure of the room: setup first, laugh later. On a feed, "laugh later" means "swiped before the laugh." The clip has to be rebuilt, and the rebuild has one rule: open at the turn, not the setup.

Start at the laugh line

Take your best minute and find the biggest laugh in it. Your clip starts one beat before that laugh: mid-bit, mid-sentence, no throat-clearing. The viewer hears a punchline and a room breaking up within three seconds, and now they have a reason to stay for the context. The feed version of your joke often runs in a different order than the stage version: proof of funny first, setup second. That feels wrong to a comic, because on stage it would be wrong. You are not on stage. You are one video in a stack of ten thousand.

This also changes which jokes you clip. A seven-out-of-ten joke with a three-second turn will outperform your nine-out-of-ten joke that needs ninety seconds of build. That is not a judgment of your act. The clip is not a ranking of your material. It is an ad for your material. Some of your best writing may never clip well, and that's fine. It will do its job in the room, for the people the clips brought there.

The caption is your opening line

A large share of viewers hit your clip with the sound off, and every viewer sees the text before they process the video. The on-screen caption and the post caption are doing the job your stage presence does live: buying you the next five seconds. Write them like jokes, because they are jokes. "POV: my landlord" is a setup. "Watch this guy realize mid-heckle that I know his wife" is a setup. "My set at the Riot last Thursday" is a filing label, and nobody stops scrolling for a filing label.

Sound sells the laugh

The single biggest production upgrade for a comedy clip is not a camera. It is audible laughter. Laughter is the proof, the social pressure, the reason a stranger decides you must be funny before they've processed the joke. A phone on a stool in the back of the room captures the PA and a wash of room noise, and the laughs die somewhere over row three. Get the phone close to the audience, or pull audio from the board, or drop a second phone near the front tables as a laugh mic. Grainy video with crisp laughter travels. Beautiful video with distant laughter dies.

Post like it's a numbers game, because it is

One clip a month tells you nothing. The comics winning online treat clips the way you treat new jokes at a mic: volume, repetition, and a short memory. Post the same bit three ways (different opening beat, different caption, different length) and let the audience tell you which cut works. You already accept this logic on stage. Nobody workshops a joke once. Clips are the same joke-writing loop running at internet speed, and every set you do generates raw material for it.
