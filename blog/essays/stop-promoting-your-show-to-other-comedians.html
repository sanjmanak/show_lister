Stop Promoting Your Show to Other Comedians

You posted the flyer three times. Forty-one likes. You know thirty-eight of those people personally, because they're comics, and two of the remaining three are on the show. On the night, nine people paid, four were comped, and the room felt like what it was: the scene performing for itself again. Afterward everyone said "good hang," which is what comics say instead of "nobody came."

Here's the mechanism underneath that night. Your social following is mostly comics. Comics follow comics. When you promote a show, the platform shows it to the people most likely to engage, which is other comics, who like the post, which teaches the platform to show your next post to even more comics. You are running a closed loop that recirculates the same forty people, and those forty people get in free everywhere. It doesn't matter how hard you promote inside that loop. You can't sell tickets to the cast.

The person you're not talking to

The person who fills comedy rooms is not in your loop. She's 31, she works in the Medical Center, it's her friend's birthday Friday, and she's deciding between a restaurant, a rooftop bar, and "something different." She has never heard of you or anyone on your lineup, and she never needs to. That is the part that should change how you write everything. She isn't buying a comedian. She's buying a night: somewhere fun, not too expensive, starts at eight, easy to get to, makes her the friend who found the cool thing.

Now look at your flyer through her eyes. Nine names she doesn't know in a grid of faces she doesn't recognize, in fonts sized by who's closing. That flyer is a cast list for a play she's never heard of. It communicates one thing to a civilian: this is for insiders. Which is exactly who shows up.

Sell the night, not the lineup

Every element of your promotion should answer her questions, not the scene's. What kind of night is it. What time does it actually start and end. What does it cost. Can we drink. Where do we park. "Comedy in the back of a taqueria, BYOB, 8 to 9:30, ten bucks, you'll be home by eleven" sells a night out. "Stacked lineup" sells nothing, because every show in town says it and she can't verify it anyway.

The competition clarifies the stakes. This week in Houston there are 63 comedy shows across 14 venues, and 15 of them are free. Your ten-dollar bar show is not competing with the other comics on Instagram for clout. It's competing with a free show, a nicer room, and a restaurant for a civilian's Friday. The show that wins is the one that made the night easiest to picture.

Promote the last show, not the next one

The best ad for Friday's show is thirty seconds of last Friday's crowd losing it. Not a graphic. A clip: real room, real laughter, the audience visible and having the night your buyer wants to have. One good crowd shot with laughter audio outsells every flyer you will ever design, because it's proof, and flyers are promises. This means the promotion for next month's show gets filmed at this month's show. If you're producing and not capturing every show, you're throwing away next month's marketing budget every time the lights go down.

Flyers still have a job: logistics. Date, time, price, address, one image that says "fun night," maybe your two most recognizable faces. That's it. The flyer confirms details for someone the clip already convinced.

Be where strangers are already looking

The civilian planning Friday doesn't open Instagram and search your name. She googles "comedy shows Houston tonight" or "things to do Houston this weekend", queries with thousands of local searches a month behind them. Check that your show is on it. If it isn't, send us the details and we'll list it. It costs you nothing, and it puts your show in front of the one audience your Instagram can't reach: strangers with plans to make.

Do this for your next show

Rewrite one promotion, just one, with the birthday group in mind instead of the green room. Lead with the night, not the names. Film this show for the next one. And spend ten minutes making sure the people already searching for comedy in Houston can find you. The scene will still see your flyer either way. The difference is whether anyone else does.
